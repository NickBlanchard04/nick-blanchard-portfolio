How to Tell If Your Local Business Website Is Costing You Customers

A website does not need to be flashy. It needs to work on a phone, explain the business clearly, feel trustworthy, and make the next step obvious.

By Nick Blanchard 7 minute read Updated July 2026

On this page

  1. Use it on a phone
  2. Find the next step
  3. Check the trust signals
  4. Read it like a customer
  5. Notice how it loads
  6. Help Google understand it

Try this review as if you have never seen the business before. A customer may be looking for a dentist between appointments, a landscaper from the driveway, or a restaurant while walking through town. They are usually moving quickly.

01

Use the website on a phone

Do not only shrink a desktop browser. Open the actual site on your phone and use it with one hand. Text should be readable without pinching. Buttons should be easy to tap. Nothing important should hang off the edge.

Quick check
  • Can you understand the first screen without zooming?
  • Can you open the menu and choose a service comfortably?
  • Can you call or contact the business in two taps or fewer?
02

Find the next step immediately

A phone number hidden in the footer is not enough. If calling is the normal next step, show a clear Call button near the top. If the business uses appointments, make Request an appointment visible and explain what happens after someone sends it.

For example, a dental office should not make a new patient search through three pages to ask for an appointment. A home-service business should not bury its estimate request beneath a long company history.

03

Check what builds—or weakens—trust

Visitors notice broken links, old hours, blurry images, and pages that look abandoned. They also notice useful details: real service information, a consistent business name, current contact details, and photos that actually belong to the business when those photos are available.

A modern design helps, but accuracy matters more. Remove expired offers. Test every contact link. Make sure the website and Google Business Profile show the same core information.

04

Read it like a customer, not the owner

The opening should answer three questions: What does this business do? Who is it for? What should I do next? “Welcome to our website” answers none of them.

Instead of

Welcome to our website. We are committed to quality and service.

Try something specific

Family dental care in Saratoga Springs, with a clear way to request your first appointment.

Use short sections, useful headings, and plain service names. A visitor should be able to scan the page and still understand the offer.

05

Notice how the site loads

Try the website away from fast Wi-Fi. Large images, autoplay video, and too many effects can make the first screen feel stuck. Compress images, load off-screen media later, and keep the most important content available even when an animation does not run.

Also check for a secure https:// address, missing images, and jumps that move buttons while the page is loading.

06

Make the business understandable to Google

Each important page should have a useful title, a clear heading, and enough original text to explain the service. The site should state the real service area naturally, use a sitemap, and be available for indexing.

A Saratoga, Lake George, or Glens Falls business does not need dozens of nearly identical city pages. One accurate service-area explanation and strong service pages are more useful than copied location text.

Foundation to look for
  • Descriptive page titles and headings
  • Consistent business name and contact details
  • A sitemap and indexing setup
  • Service and location language written for people first

The simple test

Can someone understand, trust, and contact the business without working for it?

If the answer is no, start with the first point where they get stuck. A clearer opening, a visible phone button, or a better mobile layout can matter more than adding another page.
